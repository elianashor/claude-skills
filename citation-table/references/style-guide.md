# Style guide — cells, chat, and transmittal text

## Cell prose

Finished prose: complete sentences, initial capitals, proper punctuation, closed quotes. Concise — summarize in your own words everywhere EXCEPT the verbatim-excerpt column; never pad ("supports the proposition squarely and in the authors' own words" asserts rather than shows — show the finding instead). No structured-abstract scaffolding (Background:/Objective:). No internal jargon, row-fate language ("escalated", "per the QC pass"), memo names, or personal names in any deliverable cell. Labels inside long cells are fine and encouraged (e.g., "Conceded limits:", "FUNDING/COI (p. 12, verbatim):") — they are content organization, not scaffolding. Where a verification caveat is required, state it plainly on the row face ("re-verify against the publisher's PDF before quoting").

Quotation typography: preserve the source's own (curly quotes, en-dashes, capitalization). An ellipsis appears only where text was actually cut. Pin cites in-line: (p. 12), (Table 3), (e123456 at 5).

## Working with the user

- Intake once, batched; then autonomous. Interim messages are progress-only, no content questions. Never end a turn on a promise; never pause awaiting acknowledgment. A new user message mid-run preempts everything: stop, read, re-plan.
- Lead with the outcome; keep chat output short by selectivity, not fragments. Readability beats brevity.
- Push back on a mistaken premise in one breath, then make the fix she asked for. If she repeats an instruction ("i'm telling you"), the earlier signals were already the instruction — execute without further argument.
- "I don't know what to do here" is permitted and preferred over improvisation — but only for genuine spec gaps; log it in the decisions memo with a safe default applied and keep moving.
- "How do I fix X" means a concrete per-instance menu (substitute source / rephrase claim / correct citation / quote-and-rebut / concede), not a framework. When she asks "what's your recommendation," give a ruling with one reason — a recommendation, not a survey — and apply it only if she adopts it (for anything in her voice or her deliverable's identity). Answer capability questions ("can you access X") directly, outside the current task's frame.
- Escalate inaccessible high-priority sources as a copy-paste pull list (title, authors, PMID/DOI, search keywords, why it matters) — the user retrieves them; intake follows.
- Names and titles of deliverables are the user's voice: recommend, but apply only what she adopts.
- Maintain the lessons folder (one lesson per file, one-line summary at top, why it mattered) when the project has one.

## Delivery conventions

When the user sets a submission deadline ("I want to submit today"), converge: finish and remove scaffolding, do not grow the artifact. Answer "is it finished / can I submit it?" with a concrete pre-flight of the actual file ending in yes or no — never with a list of philosophical caveats (the table being ahead of the report draft is NOT a defect of the table). When asked for a completion estimate, give one (hours/days, with the driver). Never hard-delete the user's files: move disposables to a clearly named archive folder ("safe to delete") and let the user do the final click.

Name the ONE file to open. Keep snapshots/working files out of her way (subfolder) or explain them unprompted. Verify the canonical file exists at the exact expected name before saying it's ready. Deliverables she will forward are fully self-contained. When the deliverable changes, update every companion document in the same pass. Provide a version legend if multiple files must exist. On "why won't it open": check rename, Office lock files, and cloud-sync placeholders before assuming corruption.

## Transmittal text (cover blurbs, email paragraphs she sends)

- Introduce the artifact first ("Attached is…" + what it is), then purpose, what was done, what it shows. A few plain sentences — not an essay, not a memo.
- NO em-dashes. Plain sentence structure. No trade-ambiguous words: "number" or "statistic", never "figure"; avoid "comprised of" ("comprises" or "consists of").
- Credentials get one clause ("every source was read in full; each row carries a verified, pin-cited quote"), not a paragraph.
- Every count checked against the artifact before sending (the "46 vs 45" rule). Every claim earned: process claims ("read in full and verified") are provable — superlatives ("the most relevant material published in the last 10 years") are not; if the user wants a stronger claim, either run the verification that earns it or supply the accurate substitute phrasing.
- Scope facts the recipient needs go in the artifact itself (a column, a Method note), not only in the email.
- When she supplies a sample sentence, it defines register and lead. "Shorter" cuts padding, never content she has named.
- Default blurb skeleton (settled across three revision rounds): (1) introduce the attachment — what it is, for what use; (2) one sentence on selection/relevance (how sources were chosen, organized by section); (3) one sentence on verification (read in full; verified, pin-cited quotes; funding and limitations noted); (4) any mandated-inclusion or special-disclosure note. Nothing else unless she names it.
- Every clause must be justifiable if she asks "what do you mean by X"; state explicitly what any claim is relative to (which draft date, which expert reports were checked). Keep "survives cite-check" (the citation is accurate as cited) rigorously separate from "carries weight/impeachment cost" (strategic exposure) — conflating them makes verified sources look broken.
- Final review pass on any outgoing text she drafts: grammar, missing words, counts against the artifact, ambiguous nouns — and confirm the attachment filename she is about to send matches the current deliverable, not an old snapshot.
- Two audiences rule: anything that may enter the record is conservative, precise, lawyer-voiced; anything for the expert's own prep is built for recall — short, hard structure, no throat-clearing.
